Every good haunted house starts the same way. A family moves in, everything seems lovely, and then, late one night, someone notices a door that was closed is now open.

Cloud chatbots are a bit like that. The front rooms are bright and welcoming: a friendly text box, a helpful answer, a "delete" button right where you'd expect it. But the house is much bigger than the rooms you can see, and you don't have the keys to most of them.

So in the spirit of the season, grab a flashlight. We're taking a tour of the parts of a cloud chatbot you don't usually see. Nothing here is a jump scare or a conspiracy theory. Every room is based on things that have really happened or that providers say right in their own policies. That's what makes it spooky.

## Room 1: The Attic, where deleted chats go to linger

You clicked delete. The conversation vanished from your sidebar. It's gone, right?

Not necessarily. Deleting a chat removes it from *your* view. What happens on the provider's side is up to their policy: copies are commonly kept for some period afterward for abuse monitoring, backups and operations. And that period isn't guaranteed either. When a court orders a company to preserve records, its normal deletion schedule can be suspended. That's exactly what happened in the *New York Times v. OpenAI* case, where OpenAI was ordered in 2025 to preserve ChatGPT logs, including ones users had deleted. We covered the details in [why most Americans don't trust AI companies with their data](/blog/using-ai-you-dont-trust).

The attic isn't evil. It's just full of boxes you thought you'd thrown away.

## Room 2: The Séance Room, where strangers read your words

Some cloud AI providers have human reviewers read a sample of conversations to improve quality and safety. This isn't hidden. It's usually in the privacy notice, sometimes alongside a plainly worded warning not to enter anything confidential you wouldn't want a reviewer to see. Google's notice for its Gemini apps, for example, has said exactly that.

Reviewed conversations are typically separated from your account name first, which helps. But "de-identified" is a slippery word when the conversation itself says *"I'm a nurse at the only hospital in a town of 4,000 and I need help writing up what happened with my patient last night."* The details you type are often more identifying than your name.

Picture it: a candle flickers, and somewhere, someone you'll never meet is reading your 2 a.m. question about that weird mole.

## Room 3: The Hall of Mirrors, where a shared link ends up on Google

In the summer of 2025, people discovered that thousands of shared ChatGPT conversations were showing up in Google search results. Users had clicked "share," and some had ticked a box making the link discoverable, without realizing that meant *search engines* could find it. Résumés, therapy-style confessions and work documents turned up for anyone who searched the right words. OpenAI removed the option quickly, but copies had already been cached and archived elsewhere.

Nobody was hacked. A conversation was just reflected one more time than its author expected, and in a hall of mirrors that's all it takes.

**The lesson:** a "share" button on a cloud service makes a public copy of something that started out private. Treat it like posting, not like sending.

## Room 4: The Basement, where your words become part of the house

Many consumer chatbots can use your conversations to help train future models unless you find the setting and opt out. The details vary by provider and plan, and they change often, which is a story of its own: we wrote about [what happens when the terms of service change](/blog/when-terms-of-service-change) after you've already handed over your data.

Training doesn't mean the model memorizes your chat word for word and repeats it to the next person. But it does mean your words have been absorbed into the foundations of something you don't own. Once something goes into the basement, there's no realistic way to dig it back out.

## Room 5: The Guest Who Never Leaves

Memory features are genuinely handy. The chatbot remembers that you're vegetarian, that you have two kids, and that you're training for a half marathon, so you don't have to repeat yourself.

It also means there's now a profile of you that grows a little with every chat. It quietly piles up facts about your health, your job, your relationships and your worries, stored on a server and connected to your account. You can usually view and clear it, if you remember it's there. Most people don't. It's the guest who's very polite, never makes a sound, and has been living in the spare room since March.

## Room 6: The Front Door Nobody Locks

The last room is the simplest. A cloud account is a door, and doors get opened. Passwords get reused, phones get left unlocked, laptops get borrowed. Anyone who gets into your account gets your entire chat history, every conversation you ever had with it, neatly organized and searchable.

You'd never leave a diary that detailed on the kitchen table. A cloud chat history is that diary, kept in someone else's house, behind a lock you share with them.

## How to exorcise a cloud chatbot

If you use cloud AI (and plenty of people do, for good reasons), you don't need a priest. You need a few settings and habits:

- **Find the training toggle** and turn it off if you don't want your chats used for training.
- **Check the memory settings.** Look at what it has remembered about you. You might be surprised.
- **Use temporary or incognito chats** for anything sensitive, where the provider offers them. Read what they actually promise; "not saved to history" isn't the same as "never stored."
- **Never share a link** to a conversation you wouldn't post publicly.
- **Strip out names and specifics** before you paste. If it's too late for that, here's [what to do if you already pasted something sensitive](/blog/pasted-sensitive-data-into-chatgpt).
- **Turn on two-factor authentication** for your AI account, the same as you would for email.

These help. But notice that every one of them is a way of managing the house, not leaving it. The rooms are still there.

## Or: live in a house with no extra rooms

Here's the thing about haunted houses: they're only scary because they're bigger than you think. Unknown rooms, unseen occupants, doors you didn't open.

An AI that runs [entirely on your own computer](/blog/local-ai-vs-cloud-ai) is a house with exactly the rooms you can see. The model is a file on your hard drive. Your conversations are saved on your drive and nowhere else. There's no attic on someone else's server, no séance room full of reviewers, no basement where your words become training data, and no share button that can wander onto Google. When you delete a chat, the only copy is the one you just deleted.

You don't have to take our word for that, either. Turn off your Wi-Fi and keep chatting. If it still works, your words have nowhere to go. ## Bonus: a few spooky prompts for Halloween night

Since you'll be at home handing out candy anyway, here are some ways to get into the spirit with an AI that doesn't mind how strange your requests get. (For more, see our [12 fun things to do with an offline AI](/blog/fun-things-to-do-with-offline-ai).)

**A campfire story:** *"Tell me a spooky but not too scary story for kids aged 8 to 11 about a lighthouse that turns on by itself every night. Build suspense slowly and end with a twist that's funny instead of frightening."*

**A haunted house you can run:** *"Help me plan a haunted-house walk-through for my garage on a $40 budget. Give me five 'rooms,' a sound effect for each and a script for the guide."*

**A costume brainstorm:** *"I have a gray hoodie, cardboard, aluminum foil and one hour. Give me ten costume ideas, ranked from easiest to most impressive."*

**A murder mystery party:** *"Write a 1920s murder mystery for six guests at a dinner party. Give each guest a character card with a secret, and tell me (and only me) who did it."*

**A ghost-story-off:** *"Let's take turns telling a ghost story one sentence at a time. You start, and make the first line as creepy as possible."*

Use real names, your kids' inside jokes and the floor plan of your actual house. It's not going anywhere.
