Every family has one. The person who gets handed a laptop before they've taken off their coat. "While you're here, can you look at something? It's been doing a thing."

If that's you, Thanksgiving is your busy season. You'll update the browser, delete 41 toolbars, find the printer that was "broken" because it was unplugged, and explain for the third year running that the cloud is not an actual cloud.

This year, add one more job to the list. It takes about half an hour and might be the most useful thing you set up all weekend.

## Why your parents need this more than you do

Here's what nobody in the family group chat says out loud: **your parents are probably already using AI.** They heard about it on the news, a neighbor showed them, or the search engine started answering questions on its own. And they're using it for exactly the things that make sense to them:

- Pasting in a letter from the insurance company to figure out what it means
- Typing out the name and dosage of a new prescription to ask about side effects
- Copying an email into a chatbot to ask, "Is this real?"
- Asking about a lump, a rash, a bill, a lawyer's letter, a confusing bank notice

That's all reasonable. It's also a list of the most sensitive things a person owns, typed into a website run by a company they've never thought about. Most people don't know that chats on cloud services can be [kept, reviewed or turned over in court](/blog/haunted-chat-history/), or that a "delete" button doesn't always mean what it says. Your parents almost certainly haven't read the privacy policy. (To be fair, neither have you.)

You're not going to stop them from asking a computer about their health. Nobody stops their parents from doing anything. But you can change where the asking happens.

An AI that [runs entirely on their own computer](/blog/run-ai-privately-no-coding/) gives them the same kind of help, and the questions never leave the house. No account to create, no password to forget, no data on anyone's server. When they ask it about their blood pressure pills, the only one who knows is the laptop.

## Before you drive over: check their computer

Spend two minutes on this now, so you don't discover a problem with a pie on your lap. Ask them (or check yourself next time you're on a video call) what computer they have.

You're looking for:

- **Windows 10 or later**, **a Mac with Apple Silicon** (M1 or newer, made from late 2020 on), or Linux
- **At least 8 GB of memory**, with 16 GB being comfortable
- **About 5 GB of free disk space**, plus room for the model

The one that catches families out: **older Intel Macs aren't supported.** If Dad's MacBook dates from 2018 and he's very proud of how well he's kept it, this isn't the project for this visit. (Here's [more on what hardware you actually need](/blog/do-you-need-a-powerful-computer-to-run-ai-locally/). The short version: most computers from the last few years are fine.)

## The five prompts to teach them

Here's the thing about setting up tech for parents: the setup isn't the hard part. The hard part is that they'll never open it again unless they know exactly what to do with it.

So don't give them a tour. Give them five sentences. Sit next to them and have *them* type each one, on something real from their own kitchen drawer.

### 1. "Read this letter and tell me what it wants from me."

The single most useful prompt for anyone over 65. Insurance explanations of benefits, Medicare notices, property tax letters, "IMPORTANT: ACTION REQUIRED" envelopes that turn out to require no action at all.

Have them attach the PDF, or paste in the text, or take a photo of it if you set up an image model. Then:

> *Read this letter and tell me in plain English: who sent it, what it wants me to do, and by when. If I don't need to do anything, say so.*

The look on your mom's face when a three-page letter turns into "You don't owe anything. This is just a record of your September doctor visit" is worth the drive.

### 2. "Explain this to me like I'm smart but not a doctor."

For prescriptions, test results, a diagnosis they nodded along to in the exam room.

> *My doctor prescribed lisinopril 10 mg. Explain what it's for, the common side effects, and what questions I should ask at my next appointment. Talk to me like I'm smart but not a doctor.*

Be clear about this one when you teach it: **it's for understanding, not for deciding.** It can help them show up to the appointment with good questions. It doesn't replace the appointment. AI models can be confidently wrong, and health is the place that matters most.

### 3. "Does this look like a scam?"

Paste in the suspicious email or text and ask:

> *Does this message look like a scam? What are the warning signs? Don't tell me to click anything.*

A local AI is good at spotting the classic patterns: urgency, odd payment methods like gift cards, a "grandson" who needs bail money, a sender address that doesn't match the company. It's a useful second opinion that's available at 11pm when you're not answering your phone.

But here's the honest caveat: **it can't check links or look anything up online**, and a well-made scam can fool it. So teach this rule with it: *if it asks for money, passwords, or gift cards, call me first, no matter what the AI says.* The AI is a speed bump, not a security guard.

### 4. "Help me write a reply."

To the HOA. To the contractor who didn't finish the deck. To the cousin about the reunion.

> *Help me write a short, polite but firm email to my contractor. He said the deck would be finished October 1st and it's still not done. I want a firm date.*

This is the one that turns occasional users into daily users. A lot of parents hate writing emails, and suddenly they don't have to fight with a blank page.

### 5. "Help me remember."

Recipes, family stories, the plot of the show they were watching. Or this one, which might be the best use of the whole weekend:

> *Interview me about my childhood, one question at a time. I'll answer and you ask a follow-up.*

Then everybody gets some peace while Grandpa tells a computer about the summer he worked at the cannery. Since it all stays on his laptop, he can be as candid as he likes about his brother.

## The fridge cheat sheet

Before you leave, write this on an index card, or better, print it in large type and stick it to the fridge or the monitor:

> **MY PRIVATE AI**
>
> **To open it:** click the [icon description] on the [desktop / dock / taskbar].
>
> **It stays on this computer.** Nothing I type goes to the internet. Nobody else sees it.
>
> **Things to ask:**
> 1. "Read this letter and tell me what it wants from me."
> 2. "Explain this to me like I'm smart but not a doctor."
> 3. "Does this look like a scam?"
> 4. "Help me write a reply to…"
> 5. "Interview me about…"
>
> **The rule:** If something asks for money, passwords or gift cards, **call [your name] first.** Even if the AI says it's fine.
>
> **It can be wrong.** Double-check anything about health or money.

Fill in the blanks for their computer. "Click the blue icon at the bottom of the screen, it looks like a speech bubble" beats "launch the application" every time.

## A few things worth knowing before you go

**Don't snoop.** Their chat history lives on their computer, and that's the point. You'll be tempted to scroll through it next visit "to see if they're using it." Ask them instead. Privacy that only applies until the kids show up isn't privacy.

**It doesn't know today's news.** A local model knows what it learned during training. It won't know about this week's recall, today's weather, or the new pharmacy hours. If your parents expect it to "look things up," set that expectation now, or they'll decide it's broken. (More on [what local models know and don't](/blog/which-local-ai-model-should-you-run/).)

One license can't be shared between your computer and theirs, so when the trial ends, your parents get their own. **Check in after a week.** Not on the AI, on them. "Did you try it on anything?" usually gets you a story about a confusing letter that turned out to be nothing, which is exactly the result you wanted.
