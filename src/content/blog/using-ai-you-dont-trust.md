Here's a statistic that should be stranger than it feels: the people who use AI the most trust the companies behind it the least.

In a Northeastern University survey of about 38,000 Americans this summer, 89% of 18-to-24-year-olds said they use AI tools. Only 30% said they trust AI companies. Across the whole country the picture is similar. A [Pew Research Center report](https://www.pewresearch.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2026/06/PI_2026.06.17_Americans-and-AI_REPORT.pdf) in June found that about 70% of Americans expect AI to make their personal information *less* secure. Just 3% expect it to become more secure.

So most people have arrived at an uneasy arrangement. They find AI genuinely useful, they use it every day, and they don't believe the companies running it have their interests at heart.

This post is about why that unease is reasonable, and why you don't have to choose between the two halves of it.

## The mood has shifted

For a couple of years, AI news was mostly about what the technology could do. This fall, a lot of it is about whether the companies can be trusted with it.

Axios [reported](https://www.axios.com/2026/10/09/ai-companies-day-after-major-attack) that AI companies are privately planning for a public backlash, and a few weeks later that Washington would [prefer AI executives talk less](https://www.axios.com/2026/10/24/ai-ceos-washington-political-liability). Bloomberg described a round of donation pledges from OpenAI, Anthropic and others as a response to [growing scrutiny](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-10-22/anthropic-openai-musk-pledge-donations-amid-ai-backlash). A [Quinnipiac poll](https://poll.qu.edu/poll-release?releaseid=3969) at the end of September found 86% of Americans support independent safety standards for AI companies, which is about as close to consensus as American polling gets.

Not all of that is about privacy. But a lot of everyday distrust comes down to a simple question: *what happens to the things I type into this?*

## Three reasons the worry isn't paranoia

You don't need a scandal to be uneasy. The ordinary, documented business of running a cloud chatbot is enough.

**1. Your conversations now help pick the ads.** OpenAI began [testing ads in ChatGPT](https://openai.com/blog/testing-ads-in-chatgpt/) in the U.S. in February for users on the Free and Go plans. The company says advertisers never see your conversations, and that sensitive topics like health and politics stay ad-free. That's a meaningful safeguard. It's also true that which ad you see is chosen partly from what you're talking about. A chatbot that runs on advertising has a reason to understand you, and that reason never goes away.

**2. Chats can end up as evidence in someone else's lawsuit.** In the *New York Times v. OpenAI* copyright case, a court ordered OpenAI in 2025 to preserve ChatGPT logs it would normally have deleted, including chats users had deleted themselves. In January, a federal judge [upheld an order](https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/when-chats-become-evidence-court-7484440/) requiring OpenAI to hand over a sample of 20 million de-identified conversations. The people who wrote those chats weren't parties to the case and weren't asked. Nobody did anything wrong here. It's simply what happens to stored data when lawyers get involved.

**3. Even big businesses are fighting over retention.** Fortune [reported](https://fortune.com/2026/10/05/openai-and-anthropic-battle-over-zero-data-retention-zdr-data-privacy-companies-look-to-open-models-and-sovereign-ai/) this month that how long AI providers keep customer data has become a real point of contention between companies and their AI vendors, and that a growing number of businesses are moving toward open models they can run on infrastructure they control. These are organizations with lawyers and negotiating power. If they don't feel comfortable, an individual with a free account has even less say.

It's worth being fair: the major AI labs employ serious privacy engineers, and many of their policies are better than the rest of the internet's. As we wrote when we looked at [what makes a privacy policy change](/blog/when-terms-of-service-change), the problem isn't that these companies are villains. It's that a promise is the only thing protecting your data, and promises depend on business models, courts and boardrooms you don't control.

## What the big companies are doing about it

Look at what businesses do when they stop trusting a vendor with their data. They don't stop using AI. They move the AI closer to home: open models, running on their own servers, where the data never leaves the building.

That's the same idea behind local AI, just at a smaller scale. Instead of a company's data center, the building is your laptop.

When you run an open-source model on your own computer, the questions that drive the distrust don't apply:

- **No ads**, because there's no ad business. You paid for the software, and that's the whole transaction.
- **No logs to subpoena** from a third party, because no third party ever received your conversations. Your chat history sits on your own drive, where you can delete it.
- **No retention policy to negotiate**, because nothing is being retained by anyone but you.
- **No policy change to watch for**, because there's no server on the other end for a new policy to apply to.

It turns "do I trust this company?" into a question you don't need to ask.

## The honest trade-off

Local AI isn't a free lunch. The models that run on a typical laptop are smaller than the giant ones in the cloud. They're excellent at everyday work, like rewriting an email, summarizing a contract, brainstorming or answering questions about [your own documents](/blog/ai-that-knows-less-on-purpose). They're less impressive at frontier-level tasks like long chains of complicated reasoning.

For most people, that's a good trade, especially for the conversations you'd least want in someone else's database: your health, your money, your job, your family. You don't need the biggest model in the world to help you word a hard email to your landlord. You need one that won't remember it for anyone else.
